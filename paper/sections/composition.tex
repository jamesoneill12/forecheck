\section{Multi-policy composition and the calibration hinge}
\label{sec:composition}

Guardrail deployments commonly run several independently-tuned policies at
once and act on the most restrictive decision -- OR-ing $k$ binary guards.
Each guard's false-positive rate compounds under that OR purely as a
function of $k$, independent of any guard getting worse. forecheck's
alternative is one calibrated probability vector over the eleven
dimensions, composed by one policy engine, decided either by per-dimension
thresholds (\texttt{threshold} mode) or by the argmin of expected cost over
the joint vector (\texttt{expected\_cost} mode).

Figure~\ref{fig:stacking} shows the independent and joint threshold
strategies' FPR climbing sharply as $k$ grows past 2, while
\texttt{expected\_cost\_joint} stays flat; the encoder's
\texttt{expected\_cost\_joint} curve, by contrast, sits near the top of the
FPR panel throughout, illustrating that the flat curve is a property of the
decoder's calibration, not of the decision rule alone.

Threshold-based independent stacking pushes the decoder's FPR from 0.015 at
$k{=}1$ to 0.966 by $k{=}3$, staying there through $k{=}8$ -- effectively
denying everything, not because any policy got worse but purely because
more were added. Deciding from the joint calibrated probability vector under
\texttt{expected\_cost} is invariant to $k$ from $k{=}2$ on
(FPR/FNR/review rate flat at 0.082/0.070/0.28): the decision depends on the
probability vector and a fixed cost table, not on how many rules mention a
dimension, so stacking more policies cannot by itself make the classifier
more conservative.

\textbf{The invariance is a calibration property, not a decision-rule
property.} The same rule on the granite-embedding-r2 encoder's
probabilities -- identical examples and policies -- settles at FPR 0.921, not
0.08 (Table~\ref{tab:stacking-full}). The decoder is well calibrated (macro
ECE 0.004 on \texttt{heldout\_family}, temperature-scaled on the calibration
split \citep{guo2017calibration,platt1999probabilistic}); the encoder is
flatter (ECE 0.049). Expected-cost composition only keeps a stack from
collapsing when the probabilities feeding it are sharp and calibrated, and
cannot be recovered by rule engineering alone on a poorly-calibrated model.

The cost table used throughout is the default severity-tier table, not
tuned to any deployment: the $k{=}1$ row already trades false positives for
false negatives relative to the F1-optimal threshold (FPR/FNR 0.058/0.170
vs.\ 0.015/0.253). The $k$-invariance claim is about the \emph{shape} of the
composition curve, not that this default cost table is right for any given
customer.
