\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deployed AI agents act with delegated authority: a support agent issues
refunds for a merchant, a coding agent pushes commits for a developer, an
operations agent grants IAM roles for an administrator. Before such an action
executes, some system must decide whether it is safe to proceed. The dominant
tool is a guardrail classifier that reads the proposed action and outputs a
harm judgment
\citep{inan2023llamaguard,padhi2024graniteguardian,zeng2024shieldgemma,ghosh2024aegis,openai2025gptosssafeguard}.

This conflates two questions. \textbf{Content risk} asks whether an action
looks dangerous on its face (deletes data, moves money, contacts an external
party) and is answerable from the action text. \textbf{Authorization risk}
asks whether \emph{this} action is something \emph{this} agent was allowed to
do, on behalf of \emph{this} principal, under the policy in force. It needs
the principal's entitlements, the agent's delegated scope, and the applicable
policy clause as structured inputs, not prose the model must infer.

We build a pre-execution risk classifier, \emph{forecheck}, whose input
contract makes these fields explicit, then remove them from the trained
model's input to see what breaks. Three of eleven risk dimensions collapse
from near-perfect to near-chance; the rest do not move
(\S\ref{sec:identity}). Prompting cannot close the gap: two zero-shot safety
models given the same policy text are at chance on exactly these dimensions
with or without identity, and so is the acting agent asked to judge its own
call (\S\ref{sec:selfjudgment}). A blind LLM judge shown only the rendered
text loses the same dimensions when identity is removed, replicating the
ablation on labels rather than model scores (\S\ref{sec:labelvalidity}).
Policy-conditioned reasoning generalizes to unseen policy \emph{phrasings}
but not unseen \emph{kinds} (\S\ref{sec:generalisation}), and composing
several classifiers' decisions, as guardrail stacks are deployed, compounds
toward denying everything unless the decision is made from the joint
calibrated probability vector (\S\ref{sec:composition}).

Finally we score the same checkers zero-shot on two published benchmarks we
did not generate, AgentDojo and InjecAgent (\S\ref{sec:external}).
Policy-conflict transfers on both. Prompt-injection did not transfer at 2B,
and tracing that failure exposed a label leak in our own generator that every
synthetic split had hidden. Fixing it and retraining turns the 2B failure into
a 0.69 AUPRC transfer and lifts both checkers from chance to 98\% on
InjecAgent's poisoned-versus-clean pairs, while the synthetic score on the
same dimension does not change. Every number outside \S\ref{sec:external} is
on synthetic data; \S\ref{sec:limitations} states what that licenses.
