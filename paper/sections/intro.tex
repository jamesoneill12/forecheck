\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deployed AI agents increasingly act with delegated authority: a support
agent issues refunds for a merchant, a coding agent pushes commits for a
developer, an operations agent grants IAM roles for an administrator.
Before such an action executes, some system must decide whether it is safe
to proceed. The dominant tool is a guardrail classifier that reads the
proposed action and outputs a harm judgment
\citep{inan2023llamaguard,padhi2024graniteguardian,zeng2024shieldgemma,ghosh2024aegis,openai2025gptosssafeguard}.

We argue this conflates two questions. \textbf{Content risk} asks whether
an action looks dangerous on its face (deletes data, moves money, contacts
an external party) and is answerable from the action text alone.
\textbf{Authorization risk} asks whether \emph{this} action is something
\emph{this} agent was allowed to do, on behalf of \emph{this} principal,
under the policy in force -- and requires the principal's entitlements, the
agent's delegated scope, and the applicable policy clause as structured
inputs, not prose the model must infer.

We build a pre-execution risk classifier, \emph{forecheck}, whose input
contract makes these fields explicit, then strip them from the same trained
model's input to see what breaks. On synthetic data, three of eleven risk
dimensions collapse from near-perfect to near-chance when identity is
removed, while dimensions already recoverable from action text alone are
unaffected. This is not a fine-tuning artifact prompting could fix: two
zero-shot safety models given the same policy text in-prompt are at chance
on exactly these dimensions, with or without identity, because they were
never trained to use it. We further test whether policy-conditioned
reasoning generalizes to unseen policy \emph{kinds} and \emph{phrasings},
and show that composing several such classifiers' decisions -- how
guardrail stacks are deployed in practice -- fails in a specific, fixable
way: per-policy thresholding compounds toward denying everything as more
policies are added, while deciding from the joint calibrated probability
vector does not. On AgentDojo banking traces we did not generate, zero-shot,
the 8B checker reaches 0.80 AUPRC on prompt-injection influence against a
0.15 base rate and the 2B checker reaches 0.85 AUPRC on policy conflict
against a 0.24 base rate, both against rule baselines at or near base rate,
while the 2B checker fails outright on injection. On InjecAgent, where the
call is held fixed and only the tool output changes, neither checker reads
the injected instruction (pair accuracy at or below chance); the policy
result transfers a second time (0.98 AUPRC against 0.46).

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) An identity-ablation diagnostic separating
which of eleven risk dimensions need delegated-authority context from
which are content lookups (\S\ref{sec:identity}); (2) evidence that two
current zero-shot safety models do not use delegated-authority context even
when it is handed to them (\S\ref{sec:identity}); (3) a policy-generalization
result showing robustness to unseen policy wording but not unseen policy
kinds (\S\ref{sec:generalisation}); (4) a multi-policy composition result
showing calibrated joint-probability decisions, not rule engineering, keep
a guardrail stack from collapsing to "deny everything"
(\S\ref{sec:composition}); (5) a comparison showing an agent asked to
judge its own action cannot recover the authorization signal an external
checker can (\S\ref{sec:selfjudgment}); and (6) an independent blind-judge
check of whether the generator's labels are recoverable from the rendered
text at all, which confirms the identity-ablation result on two of its three
load-bearing dimensions and flags the third as unvalidated
(\S\ref{sec:labelvalidity}); (7) zero-shot transfer results on
AgentDojo and InjecAgent, two published external benchmarks, showing that
policy-conflict transfers to agent traces and policies forecheck never saw,
that the injection result is call-level rather than a reading of untrusted
text, that unauthorized-scope does not transfer, and that calibration needs
a few hundred in-domain labels (\S\ref{sec:external}); (8) a label-leak
diagnosis of our own generator that invalidates every synthetic
prompt-injection number before v6 (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). Every number here except \S\ref{sec:external} is on
synthetic data from forecheck's own scenario generator; \S\ref{sec:limitations}
states plainly what that does and does not license.
