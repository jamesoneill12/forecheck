\title{Delegated Authority Is an Input, Not an Inference: \\ Diagnosing and Externally Validating a Pre-Execution Risk Checker for Tool-Using Agents}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Guardrail models for AI agents classify a proposed tool call from its text.
We argue that pre-execution risk for delegated agents has a second component,
authorization, whose inputs are the principal's entitlements, the agent's
delegated scope, and the policy in force, and we build a checker whose typed
input contract carries them. Removing those fields from a trained 2B checker
drops macro AUPRC from 0.96 to 0.79 on held-out tools, with the loss confined
to the three labels that are functions of the removed fields; the residual
content signal on two of them is nil, an 8B checker and a train-time ablation
behave identically, and a blind LLM judge loses the same two labels when the
fields are removed from its input. Two zero-shot safety models and the acting
agent, given the same policy text, do not use it. Policy checking generalises
to unseen phrasings but not unseen policy kinds. An apparent collapse of
multi-policy threshold stacking turns out to be one fail-closed bundle, and
we correct our own earlier reading of it. The main evidence is external: on
AgentDojo and InjecAgent, traces we did not generate, policy checking against
predicates we wrote transfers zero-shot, while prompt-injection detection did
not until the external failure exposed a label leak in our generator that
every synthetic split had scored at its ceiling. Retrained leak-free, the
checkers rank the poisoned InjecAgent observation above its clean twin on
98\% of pairs; auditing that construction then surfaced a length confound we
could not rule out. We report the confounds alongside the results and argue
for evaluating on data one did not generate, and then auditing that too.
\end{abstract}
